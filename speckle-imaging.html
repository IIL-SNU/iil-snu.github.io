Research/Dynamic Speckle Imaging

Dynamic speckle imaging

IIL develops dynamic speckle imaging, which analyzes speckle from dynamic scattering to measure the dynamics of a sample, such as blood flow, with standard image sensors.

Summary

We analyze speckle produced by dynamic scattering to measure the dynamics of a sample. When laser light enters living tissue, moving red blood cells make the speckle pattern fluctuate, at a rate set by the flow speed. Measuring the fluctuation directly usually requires cameras running at more than ten thousand frames per second. Rolling-shutter speckle imaging (RSSI) uses the row-by-row exposure of CMOS sensors, in which successive rows are exposed microseconds apart, so that a single frame records the speckle dynamics. With elongated speckles and a physical model, one frame gives both the flow speed and the fraction of moving scatterers. Applications include cerebral blood-flow mapping, pulse waveforms at the fingertip and real-time analysis with physics-guided neural networks.

2022–2023

Rolling-shutter speckle imaging (RSSI)

Laser speckle imaging maps blood flow without dyes, but quantitative measurements require following how the speckle decorrelates over microseconds, which normally means costly cameras running above ten thousand frames per second. RSSI instead uses the row-by-row exposure of CMOS sensors, in which successive rows are exposed microseconds apart.

  • C. Yi et al., Optica 9, 1227 (2022)
  • C. Yi et al., Proc. SPIE PC11974, Biomedical Applications of Light Scattering XII (2022)
  • C. Yi et al., Optica Imaging Congress (COSI), CW5B.7 (2023)

2023–2024

A complete spatiotemporal speckle-correlation model

A single RSSI frame becomes a measurement only once we know how the row-by-row correlation should behave for a given flow, in tissue where moving blood is mixed with static scatterers. Our first model assumed an instantaneous exposure, but real sensors integrate light for longer than the delay between rows.

  • C. Yi et al., Biomed. Opt. Express 15, 1253 (2024)
  • C. Yi et al., Proc. SPIE PC12853, High-Speed Biomedical Imaging and Spectroscopy IX (2024)

2023–2024

Rolling-shutter speckle plethysmography (RSSPG)

Photoplethysmography (PPG), the green light on the back of a smartwatch, tracks how much blood lies under the sensor but not how fast it moves. RSSPG applies rolling-shutter speckle imaging to a sequence of frames of a fingertip and extracts, frame by frame, both the decorrelation time, which follows flow speed, and the dynamic scattering fraction, which follows blood volume.

  • Y. Lee et al., Biomed. Opt. Express 15, 1540 (2024)
  • Y. Lee et al., Optica Imaging Congress (COSI), CW5B.2 (2023)

2024–2026

Physics-guided neural networks for real-time speckle imaging

Single-shot RSSI trades statistics for speed: each map is estimated from relatively few speckle grains, so pixel-wise curve fitting is both noisy and slow. Averaging many frames reduces the noise but gives up the time resolution of single-shot imaging.

  • S. Byun et al., J. Phys. Photonics 8, 045002 (2026)
  • S. Byun et al., Proc. SPIE PC13333, Computational Optical Imaging and Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Sciences II (2025)

2025–, ongoing

Speckle-based eye-movement tracking

Fast eye movements, from saccades to the tiny microsaccades made while fixating, carry information about attention and neurological health, yet capturing them usually calls for high-speed cameras. We are extending rolling-shutter speckle imaging to the eye: laser light scattered from the white of the eye (the sclera) forms elongated speckles on a rolling-shutter sensor.

  • S. Byun et al., “Rapid eye movement detection via rolling shutter speckle imaging,” SPIE-ABC 2025, Jeju (poster)

2025

Deep-tissue blood flow with massively parallel DCS

Speckle fluctuations also reveal blood flow far below the surface. Diffuse correlation spectroscopy (DCS) measures how laser light flickers after diffusing through centimeters of tissue, but in adults the light that reaches the brain, beneath scalp and skull, returns faint and noisy.
