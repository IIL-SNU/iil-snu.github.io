Research/Lensless Cameras and Learned Optics

Lensless cameras and learned optics

IIL develops lensless cameras and learned optics: thin phase masks designed and fabricated for each application, and reconstruction algorithms that combine physical models with deep learning.

Summary

A conventional camera needs a stack of lenses, which sets a lower limit on its size, weight and cost. A lensless camera replaces the lenses with a thin patterned mask just above a bare image sensor. The mask encodes the scene into a pattern on the sensor, and the image is reconstructed computationally. We design the camera hardware for each application and develop the reconstruction algorithms: masks with designed point-spread functions are made in a single lithography step, also directly on the sensor, and physics-guided neural networks reconstruct the images. Applications include polarization, hyperspectral and eye-tracking cameras, and privacy-preserving imaging.

Custom phase masks with designer point-spread functions

In a lensless camera, the mask's surface relief sets the point-spread function (PSF), the pattern that a single point of light forms on the sensor. Early lensless cameras relied on off-the-shelf diffusers or on masks that were slow and costly to make.

  • K. C. Lee, J. Bae, N. Baek, J. Jung, W. Park, S. A. Lee, Optica 10(1), 72–80 (2023). Cover article

2022

Integrated on-chip lensless camera by UV-imprint lithography

A phase mask must sit at a precise distance above the sensor; a small spacing error blurs the PSF and degrades the reconstruction. Instead of aligning a separate mask, we replicate it directly on the sensor. A PDMS mold taken from a master phase mask is pressed into a layer of photocurable resin on the image sensor, and the relief is fixed with UV light.

  • Y. Lee, H. Chae et al., IEEE Photonics Journal 14(2), 1–8 (2022). Editor's Pick
  • Y. Lee et al., Imaging and Applied Optics Congress (COSI) 2022, CTh4C.7

2022

Snapshot full-Stokes lensless polarization camera

Polarization reveals stress in plastics, birefringence in biological tissue and surface properties that ordinary color images miss. Most commercial polarization cameras pattern tiny linear polarizers over the pixels, so they cannot see circular polarization, and full-Stokes instruments are usually bulky.

  • N. Baek, Y. Lee, T. Kim, J. Jung, S. A. Lee, APL Photonics 7, 116107 (2022)

2022–2023

Aperture-encoded snapshot hyperspectral lensless camera

Hyperspectral imaging records a full spectrum at every pixel, which helps identify materials and tissue types, but most systems scan over time or need bulky dispersive optics. We placed a linear variable filter, whose passband shifts with position, at the aperture of a phase-mask lensless camera.

2020–2025

Learned reconstruction: from end-to-end networks to diffusion priors

Classical lensless reconstruction solves an optimization problem for every frame, which is slow and leaves characteristic artifacts. In one of the lab's first lensless papers, we trained an encoder–decoder (U-Net) network to map raw measurements from our phase-mask camera directly to images.

2025–2026

IFIN: Integrated Forward–Inverse Network

Lensless measurements are heavily multiplexed: every pixel mixes light from across the scene, so reconstruction is ill-conditioned and sensitive to calibration errors and model mismatch. Hybrid networks that apply a physics-based deconvolution once and then hand the result to a generic neural network tend to lose track of the measurement deeper in the network.

  • D. Bae et al., Integrated Forward–Inverse Network for Lensless Image Reconstruction, ECCV 2026 (project page)
  • arXiv:2607.04608

2025–2026

Geometry-aware wave-optics modeling for mask design

Designing optics by gradient descent needs a wave-optics simulator that is accurate, differentiable and fast. The standard angular spectrum method (ASM) needs dense lateral grids and many thin axial slices to model thick refractive elements and steep incidence angles, which quickly becomes too expensive for wide-angle, large-aperture designs.

  • Y. G. Kang et al., Optics Express 34(8), 15244 (2026)

2019–2024

High-speed lensless eye tracker for microsaccades

Microsaccades, tiny involuntary eye movements that occur even while we fixate, may carry early signs of Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, but measuring them usually takes high-speed, lab-grade eye trackers. Our goggle-type tracker pairs a phase mask with an inexpensive rolling-shutter CMOS sensor and infrared illumination.

  • T. Kim et al., High-speed lensless eye tracker for microsaccade measurement, Proc. SPIE 13076, SPIE Advanced Biophotonics Conference 2023 (published 2024)

2025–, ongoing

Privacy-preserving and task-optimized lensless sensors

A lensless camera does not have to produce pictures for people. Two directions we presented at the Optica Imaging Congress (COSI 2025) treat the mask as a design variable for what a camera should, and should not, reveal. Privacy by design: an ordinary lensless camera has a shift-invariant PSF, so its raw data can be inverted by anyone with enough example pairs.

  • K. C. Lee et al., Privacy-Preserving Imaging with Lensless Cameras Using Shift-Variant Point Spread Functions, Optica Imaging Congress (COSI) 2025, CTh1B.4
  • T. Kim, J. Kim, J. Jung, S. A. Lee, Task-Optimized Lensless Imaging with Trainable Phase Masks for Compact Spatial Encoding, Optica Imaging Congress (COSI) 2025, CTh1B.5
