Research/Computational Microscopy

Computational microscopy: Fourier ptychography, phase and polarization imaging, virtual staining

The Imaging Intelligence Laboratory led by Seung Ah Lee at Seoul National University develops computational microscopes based on Fourier ptychographic microscopy (FPM) for wide-field, high-resolution and quantitative phase imaging, including a smartphone FPM (K. C. Lee et al., ACS Photonics 2021), FPM that uses an OLED display as the light source (K. Lee et al., Optics and Lasers in Engineering 2024), single-shot FPM for quantitative phase video (H. Yoon et al., Applied Physics Letters 2025) and polarization-resolved (vectorial) FPM for birefringence imaging (H. Chae et al., Laser & Photonics Reviews 2026). The same microscopes are used for virtual staining of unstained tissue slides (H. Chae et al., APL Photonics 2024).

Summary

A conventional microscope forces a trade-off: zoom in for detail and you lose the big picture, and transparent cells barely show up at all. Fourier ptychography sidesteps both limits. We light a sample from many angles with an LED array or a display screen, record a series of low-resolution images through an inexpensive, low-magnification lens, and let an algorithm stitch them together in Fourier space. The result is a wide-field, high-resolution image that also recovers the optical phase, revealing unstained cells and tissue in quantitative detail. We push this idea toward smaller, faster and richer instruments: smartphone microscopes, single-shot video-rate imaging and polarization-resolved maps of birefringence.

2019–2021

Smartphone Fourier ptychographic microscope

Smartphones already carry an excellent camera, a bright programmable display and a fast processor. We combined all three into a stand-alone Fourier ptychographic microscope. A compact 3D-printed optics module guides light from the phone's own screen through the sample and into its built-in camera, so lighting up individual spots on the display becomes a programmable, angle-scanning illumination source.

2020–2022

Live-cell phase imaging inside the incubator

Watching living cells usually means choosing between a wide view and fine detail, and often adding fluorescent labels that can disturb the cells. We built a miniaturized Fourier ptychographic microscope small enough to operate inside a standard CO₂ cell-culture incubator, using a low-magnification 4× objective, a machine-vision camera and an inexpensive LED matrix.

Display screens as programmable FP illumination

Fourier ptychography is usually lit by an LED array, whose millimetre-scale LEDs must sit far from the sample to act like clean plane waves. Modern OLED displays offer something better: millions of micrometre-sized emitters that can be switched on in any pattern.

Noise-robust FP with anisotropic regularization

Standard FP needs many heavily overlapping images with good signal, which slows acquisition and limits low-cost hardware such as phone-based microscopes. When measurements are sparse or noisy, the inverse problem becomes ill-posed and conventional algorithms produce artifacts.

2023–2025

Single-shot Fourier ptychography at video rate

FP's need for many sequential images makes it too slow for moving samples. We encode several illumination angles into a single exposure: linear polarizers at four orientations tag different groups of LEDs, and a polarization camera, whose pixels separate the four polarization channels, records them all at once.

  • H. Yoon, H. Chae, K. C. Lee, K. Lee, S. A. Lee, Appl. Phys. Lett. 126, 123702 (2025)

2009–2014

Chip-scale lensless microscopy (PhD roots at Caltech)

Before the lab existed, Prof. Lee's PhD work at Caltech (in Changhuei Yang's group) asked how small and inexpensive a microscope could be. In chip-scale microscopy the sample sits directly on a CMOS image sensor and casts a shadow onto its pixels; sequences of slightly shifted shadow images are then combined by pixel super-resolution to beat the pixel-size limit.

Computational Microscopy

Virtual staining

Within computational microscopy, Seung Ah Lee's laboratory develops phase-to-stain virtual staining for digital pathology: Fourier ptychographic microscopes measure the quantitative phase of unstained tissue slides, and neural networks convert it into images of chemically stained slides. Published work covers the restoration of faded H&E slides (H. Chae et al., APL Photonics 2024) and fast virtual staining with learned illumination (K. C. Lee et al., Journal of Physics: Photonics 2026).

Summary

Pathologists read tissue through chemical stains such as hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Staining is slow, consumes tissue and reagents, varies from lab to lab, and fades over the years. Unstained tissue is nearly transparent, but it still delays light by an amount set by its local density and thickness. Our Fourier ptychographic microscopes measure this optical phase quantitatively across whole slides, and deep neural networks learn to translate it into the colors of a stained slide. We use this to restore faded archival slides digitally, to co-design illumination and networks so that a slide region is scanned in seconds, and to explore stains beyond H&E, working with pathologists at Severance Hospital.

Restoring faded slides with NIR phase-to-color virtual staining

Hematoxylin and eosin stains fade over time, so archived slides that may be needed again for diagnosis or research slowly lose their information. Physical re-staining is laborious and can damage the tissue, and digital color normalization fails once the stain is nearly gone.

2022–2025

High-speed virtual staining with learned illumination

For virtual staining to fit a clinical workflow, two bottlenecks have to go: collecting perfectly paired stained and unstained training images is costly, and capturing hundreds of FP images per field is slow. We tackled both with a hardware-software co-designed system.

  • K. C. Lee et al., J. Phys. Photonics 8, 015025 (2026)
  • Code: github.com/IIL-SNU/FPVS

2024–, ongoing

Beyond H&E: unpaired, immunohistochemical and 3D virtual staining

Supervised virtual staining depends on pixel-matched image pairs, which are hard to obtain for every tissue type and stain. We are developing unsupervised approaches that learn from unpaired collections of label-free phase images and existing whole-slide images of stained tissue.
